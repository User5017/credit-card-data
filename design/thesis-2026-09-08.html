US card credit is re-segmenting, not cycling

Analysis note, 2026-09-08. Every figure comes from the dashboard's own loaded data (data/facts.csv) as of the 2026-09-08 refresh. Large-bank Y-14 data runs to 2026 Q1; everything else to 2026 Q2 or later. Written after adding the macro context series and the NY Fed credit access survey specifically to test this.

The 2022 to 2024 deterioration in US card credit was a vintage and underwriting event, not a macroeconomic one, and it has largely resolved. What has not reverted is the price. Issuers took roughly five points of margin over prime during the loss cycle and have kept it, while the product itself split into a growing payments business and a shrinking lending business. Lenders are now reopening the front door to subprime borrowers on very small lines while closing more existing weak accounts than at any point in the survey's history. This is portfolio re-segmentation, not a credit cycle turning.

1. The loss cycle was a vintage event and it is over

Every loss measure on the dashboard peaked between late 2023 and early 2025 and has fallen since. None has returned to its pre-pandemic average.

The stronger evidence is that losses were never explicable by the economy. Fitting the card charge-off rate on the unemployment rate across 166 quarters back to 1985 gives

charge-off rate = 0.468 × unemployment rate + 1.590

and the residual is the part the labour market does not explain. It ran between +0.88 and +1.18 points through 2024 and 2025 Q1. It is now +0.26 points.

Unemployment barely moved across the whole episode: 3.9% to 4.4%. A loss surge that appears and then disappears without the labour market moving is not a macroeconomic event. It is the 2021 and 2022 origination vintages, opened into pandemic-inflated credit scores and stimulus-flattered payment histories, seasoning and then washing out.

2. There is no aggregate debt problem

The headline that card balances are at a record is true in nominal dollars and misleading in every other unit.

Card debt is a smaller claim on household income than at any point before the pandemic, and 40 percent smaller than in 2008. In constant dollars, balances are 15 percent below their 2008 peak. The cost of servicing all consumer debt is below its 2019 average despite card rates being seven points higher, because the balances carrying those rates have not grown in real terms.

3. The product has split into payments and lending

This is the finding that reframes everything else, and it comes from series that were already loaded before this analysis started. The Y-14 accounting identity gives a payment rate: payments as a share of the opening balance.

The payment rate rose for seven straight years before the pandemic, jumped through it, and has given back only a fraction. Cardholders now put 47 percent more spend per account through the product than in 2019 and pay off almost all of it. The share of accounts paying in full is 36.9 percent, the highest in the series; the share paying only the minimum is 10.2 percent, below its 2024 peak. Utilisation among active accounts has a median of 6.8 percent.

For an issuer this is good credit and poor spread revenue. The asset that earns interest is shrinking as a share of a book whose transaction volume is growing. The economics shift toward interchange, and the interest-earning balance concentrates in a smaller, worse-performing tail.

4. The repricing was permanent

Most variable card APRs are set as the prime rate plus a margin. Prime tracks monetary policy; the margin is the issuer's decision. The margin is at a record.

Roughly five points of margin were added between 2015 and today, on top of the four points added between 2004 and 2014. The margin peaked at 15.47 points in 2025 Q4, when losses were already falling, and it has not come back. Note what has moved instead: the CFPB survey shows median APRs offered to new applicants falling in the second half of 2025, from 28.2 to 26.7 percent for the 620 to 719 band and from 22.2 to 20.0 percent for superprime, while the rate actually paid on existing balances did not move. Competition is showing up in acquisition pricing and not in back-book pricing, which is the textbook signature of a market where switching costs are high.

5. The front door is open and the back door is closing

Two independent sources agree that supply loosened sharply between mid-2025 and early 2026.

Refusal rates are back to 2019 levels and limit refusals are below them. But three things say this is not a risk-on expansion.

Lender-initiated closures are at a series high

The share of households that had an account closed by the lender is 8.3 percent on a three-wave average, the highest since the survey began in 2013, against 6.3 percent in 2019. For households with a self-reported score under 680 it is 22.8 percent, against 16.0 percent in 2019. Y-14 line decreases are at 1.65 percent of accounts, the highest since 2020. No lender-reported source publishes this; it is visible only because the survey asks households directly.

The money is not following the accounts

Sub-660 borrowers are 19.1 percent of new accounts but only 4.0 percent of new credit line dollars, against 6.0 percent in 2019. The median credit line on a new sub-660 account is $500, down from $700 through 2021 to 2024. The median new superprime line is $7,500, up from $5,295 in 2019. Lenders are opening subprime accounts as cheap options and concentrating actual credit on prime borrowers who do not borrow.

Account growth is decelerating even as limits grow

Open card accounts on credit reports grew 7.0 percent year over year in 2025 Q3 and 2.6 percent in 2026 Q1. Total limits grew 6.1 percent over the same period. Limits per account are rising because closures are removing small accounts while lines grow on the ones that remain.

6. What follows, and what would prove it wrong

If the loosening that began in the second half of 2025 holds, those vintages season into delinquency between late 2026 and 2027. The dashboard can test this.

Prediction. Delinquency rates rise modestly through 2027 while dollar losses stay near current levels, because the new subprime exposure is being written at a $500 median line. Specifically: the NY Fed flow into 90+ day delinquency, flat between 6.93 and 7.18 percent for six quarters, ticks up but stays below 7.5 percent, while the Fed charge-off rate stays under 4.2 percent.

What would falsify the thesis. The 90+ flow breaking above 7.5 percent, or the charge-off excess over what unemployment implies widening back above 0.75 points, would mean the loosening was larger than the line sizes suggest and this is a cycle after all. A margin over prime falling below 14 points would mean the repricing was cyclical rather than structural.

Two other things to watch. The concentration is extreme and is not narrowing: annualised charge-off rates run from American Express at 2.3 percent through JPMorgan at 3.2 and Capital One at 4.8 to Bread at 7.1, Credit One at 11.8 and Merrick at 13.3. Banks outside the largest 100 have a 30+ delinquency rate of 6.49 percent against 2.74 percent for the top 100. And borrowers aged 70 and over have a serious delinquency flow that has risen from 5.33 to 6.34 percent since 2023, the only age band still deteriorating.

7. What the data cannot settle

  • Why older borrowers keep deteriorating. The NY Fed publishes the delinquency flow by age but not balances by age, so composition cannot be separated from distress. The credit access survey shows households aged 60 and over have the lowest rejection rate of any age band, 6.1 percent, so it is not a supply story.
  • Whether the sentiment gap means anything. Consumer sentiment is 55.2 against a 2015-19 average of 95.2, with a record low of 44.8 in May 2026, and the saving rate is 3.0 percent against 6.1 percent. Households say they are in trouble and simultaneously pay their cards off at record rates. Both are true of different people, but nothing here measures how many are in each group.
  • Timing. Call reports arrive 55 days after quarter end and the Y-14 three and a half months after. Monthly issuer 8-K master trust data would cut that to about two weeks and is the single highest-value addition left.
  • Sample size. The survey's sub-680 credit score band rests on about 150 respondents a wave. Every figure quoted from it here is a three-wave average for that reason, and single-wave moves of several points in that cell are noise.
