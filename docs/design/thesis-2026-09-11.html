Cards turned down while every other debt turned up

Analysis note, 2026-09-11. The second note, revising the note of 2026-09-08, which stands as written and is not edited. Every figure comes from the dashboard's own loaded data (data/facts.csv) as of the 2026-09-11 refresh. Written after adding nine more sources and, in the last day, Capital One's monthly 8-K metrics, the Fed's distributional accounts, the state-level panel, the other five loan types in the NY Fed workbook, and the cross-asset lending rates. Those were added to test the first note, not to decorate it, and one of them cost it a leg.

Three days ago this dashboard argued that the card loss cycle was a vintage event that had washed out, that the repricing was permanent, and that the market was re-segmenting rather than cycling. The data added since strengthens the first two claims and puts a real constraint on the third. It also turned up something the first note could not see: cards are now the only consumer debt getting better. The flow into serious delinquency has fallen on cards since the end of 2024 and risen on mortgages, home equity lines and all debt taken together. The card story was never the household story, and the two have now separated.

1. The cycle turning is now visible six weeks earlier, and it is real

The first note rested on quarterly series that arrive late. Everything it said about a single lender came from the FDIC call report, 55 to 58 days after quarter end, or the Y-14, about three and a half months after. Capital One publishes its own charge-off and delinquency rates in an 8-K about two weeks after each month ends. Those 66 months are now loaded, and they are the freshest read on the argument.

The monthly series is not seasonally adjusted and card losses peak in the first quarter, so the fall from 5.17 percent in February to 4.12 percent in July is partly a seasonal move and must not be read as the whole story. The comparison that controls for it is the same month a year apart, and every month of 2026 is below its 2025 twin:

Six out of six is not a trend read off a chart; it is the same calendar month improving every time. The quarterly sources agree and are simply slower: the Fed's all-bank card charge-off rate is 3.82 percent against a 4.69 percent peak in 2024 Q3, and the NY Fed's flow into 90+ is 6.97 percent against 7.18 percent at the end of 2024.

One number in this table does not improve as fast, and it is the leading one. Capital One's 30+ day delinquency rate, which is what turns into charge-offs six months later, went from 3.67 percent in July 2025 to 3.48 percent in July 2026. That is a fall of 0.19 points against 0.71 points on charge-offs. Charge-offs are working off a stock of delinquency that built in 2024; the inflow is improving about a quarter as fast. If the argument has to be defended in six months, this is the number that will decide it, not the charge-off rate.

2. Cards are the worst consumer asset by far, and the only improving one

The first note compared cards only with cards. The NY Fed workbook the pipeline already downloaded carries five other loan types, and reading them changes the picture in both directions.

First, the level. Cards are not merely the worst-performing consumer asset; nothing else is close:

A credit card balance is thirteen times more likely to be seriously delinquent than a mortgage balance belonging to the same population of households. That is the strongest single piece of evidence that card distress is a card phenomenon rather than a household one, which is what the first note claimed and could not then show.

Second, and cutting the other way, the direction. Between the card peak at the end of 2024 and 2026 Q2, cards improved and the secured books deteriorated. Mortgage flow went from 1.09 to 1.52, home equity from 0.56 to 1.15, auto is flat to slightly worse. The first note's sentence that every loss measure had turned was true of cards and is not true of household debt.

Two things this table must not be used to say. The student loan column is an administrative artefact, not distress: paused loans could not go delinquent under the federal payment pause, so the series is flat from 2020 to 2023 and then steps up as reporting restarted in 2024. The 0.70 to 7.83 move is the reporting switch being thrown. And the mortgage and home equity rises are off a floor so low they are almost rounding: a 0.99 percent mortgage 90+ share compares with 8.89 percent at the worst of the financial crisis. This is not a housing cycle. It is the observation that the direction of travel is no longer the same across the balance sheet.

3. The buffer behind the most exposed half is better than it was in 2019

The first note argued there was no aggregate debt problem using economy-wide ratios, and conceded that an aggregate says nothing about who holds the debt. The Fed's Distributional Financial Accounts answer that directly.

The half of households that owes most of the consumer credit owes a slightly smaller share of it than before the pandemic, holds more cash against it, and owes it against roughly twice the net worth. Every one of those moves is in the direction of resilience. This is the leg that has strengthened most, and it is the reason to expect the delinquency improvement to continue rather than to be a pause.

4. The margin is a card decision, and its own controls prove it

The first note showed the card margin over prime at a record 15.40 points and argued it was an issuer decision rather than a funding cost. The objection to that argument was obvious: if funding costs or credit risk had risen for everyone, every consumer loan rate would have risen together. The G.19 survey prices two other consumer loans, at the same banks, in the same survey, in the same quarter:

Same survey, same banks, 2026 Q2RateOver prime
Credit card, accounts assessed interest22.15%15.40 pts
Personal loan, 24 month11.86%5.11 pts
New car loan, 48 month7.47%0.72 pts
Prime rate6.75%—

The card rate is nearly twice the personal loan rate and three times the car loan rate at the same institutions. Whatever the card margin is compensating for, it is not the cost of money, because the same banks lend the same households money at 11.86 percent when it is a personal loan. Some of the gap is real: cards are unsecured, revolving and carry a 12.92 percent serious delinquency rate against a car loan's 5.49. But the loss side does not close it. The control was the missing piece of this leg and it holds.

5. What the geography costs the thesis

"Re-segmenting" invites a reading in which distress concentrated somewhere: in particular places, particular lenders, particular vintages. The state panel, added after the first note, tests the first of those and rejects it.

Card delinquency rose in every one of the 51 areas with a full history between 2021 and 2025, by a median of 3.8 points, and by at least 2.3 points in the area that moved least. There is no state that sat the episode out.

Lowest area5.47%5.34%8.03%
Median area7.42%7.38%11.35%

Range6.04 pts6.66 pts8.25 pts

The best-performing state in 2025, at 8.03 percent, is worse than the median state was in 2019. The episode was national. What did happen is that the spread widened, from 6.04 points in 2019 to 8.25 in 2025, and the ordering barely moves from year to year: Nevada has been in the worst sixth of areas in every year since 2003.

So the thesis has to be stated more precisely than the first note stated it. The re-segmentation is across borrowers, not across places. It shows up as a widening gap between lenders serving different borrowers, which the data does support: banks outside the largest 100 have a card delinquency rate of 6.49 percent against 2.74 for the top 100, and annualised charge-off rates at 2026 Q2 run from American Express at 2.30 percent through JPMorgan at 3.15 and Capital One at 4.83 to Bread Financial at 7.13, Credit One at 11.81 and Merrick at 13.28. A reader who took "re-segmenting" to mean the map would light up unevenly was reading more than the data supports, and the first note did not guard against that reading.

6. The prediction, unchanged, and what would falsify it

The three falsification thresholds are carried forward from the first note deliberately and without adjustment. Moving a goalpost while the ball is in the air is how a thesis becomes unfalsifiable, and the dashboard evaluates these on every render, so a change would be visible in the git history.

The prediction is also unchanged: delinquency rates rise modestly through 2027 while dollar losses stay near current levels, because the new subprime exposure is being written at a $500 median line.

What this second note adds is a faster tripwire. The quarterly thresholds above are confirmed months after the fact. Capital One's monthly series will break first, and the honest statement of what would worry me is: three consecutive months in which Capital One's charge-off rate is above its year-earlier month. That has not happened once in 2026. It would say the improvement had stopped roughly two quarters before the NY Fed flow could say it.

One more thing would falsify a claim this note makes rather than the first one: if the mortgage and home equity flows keep rising and the card flow keeps falling for another year, "cards are the only debt improving" becomes a statement about a turning household cycle rather than a divergence, and the thesis would need to explain why the unsecured book leads on the way down as well as on the way up.

7. What the data still cannot settle

Answered since the first note: why older borrowers keep deteriorating

The first note listed this as unexplained. The by-age sheets for the other loan types settle it. For households headed by someone 70 or over, the flow into serious delinquency is 6.34 percent on cards, 2.45 percent on auto loans and 1.80 percent across all their debt. The deterioration is card-specific, not a balance-sheet-wide problem for older households. Combined with the distributional accounts showing their consumer credit per household flat since 2023, the composition reading is the stronger one: this is about which older households hold cards, not about older households falling apart.

Answered since the first note: the timing gap

The first note called monthly issuer data "the single highest-value addition left". It is now loaded, for one issuer. Capital One carries the largest card book in the FDIC data, $250.5bn at 2026 Q2 against JPMorgan's $216.2bn and an industry $1,189.6bn, so it is about a fifth of card loans at insured institutions. It is still one lender with a distinctive book, and a single issuer improving is weaker evidence than an industry improving. Synchrony, Bread Financial and American Express publish comparable monthly figures and are not loaded; nothing here measures any issuer's share of the subprime card market, so no claim about that is made.

Still open: whether the sentiment gap means anything

Unchanged from the first note. Consumer sentiment is far below its 2015-19 average and the saving rate is half it, while cardholders pay off record shares of their balances. Both are true of different people and nothing loaded here measures how many are in each group.

Still open: the scope mismatch between the two best issuer sources

Capital One's own July 2026 charge-off rate is 4.12 percent and the FDIC's rate for the same issuer at 2026 Q2 is 4.83 percent. Both are correct: the 8-K covers the managed domestic card book and the call report covers what sits on the bank charter. The two cannot be compared level for level, which means the monthly series is a good read on direction and a bad one on level. Nothing loaded reconciles them.

Still open: what the 2025 loosening actually bought

The first note's fifth leg, that the front door is open while the back door closes, has not been retested here. The credit access survey is four-monthly and the next wave is the test.
